1What there is and there are are for

We use there is and there are to say that something exists, or to introduce something for the first time. It is how English answers the question “What is in this place?”1

  • There is a park in our town.
  • There are three shops on this street.
  • There is a problem with my phone.
The word there here is not a place In “Look over there!” the word there means a place. In “There is a park” it means nothing at all — it is simply the word English puts at the front so the sentence has a beginning. The real subject is a park, and it comes after the verb. This is why the verb seems to agree with the wrong word: it is agreeing with the noun behind it.

Once the thing is introduced, we stop using there is and switch to it or they:

  • There is a park near my school. It is very big and it has a lot of trees.
  • There are two cafes on this street. They are both open until late.

The question turns around

To make a question, swap the two words. Nothing else changes, and you do not need do or does:

  • There is a hospital. → Is there a hospital?
  • There are any shops. → Are there any shops?

not any and no

There are two ways to make the negative. No is a little stronger, and often sounds more final:1

  • There isn't any bread.  =  There is no bread.
  • There aren't any buses after ten.  =  There are no buses after ten.

Use one or the other, never both. There isn't no bread is a double negative and is not correct in standard English.

From Week 4 some and any behave here exactly as they did with food: some in positive sentences, any in negatives and questions. The grammar you learned last week is still working.

3Choosing is or are

Look at the noun after the verb, not at the word there. That noun decides everything.

there is one thing · or something uncountable
There is a bridge.
There is one school.
There is some water.
There is a lot of traffic.
Uncountable nouns are singular, however large the quantity sounds.
there are two or more things
There are two bridges.
There are many people.
There are some trees.
There are a lot of cars.
If you can put a number in front of the noun, you need are.

When a list follows

In careful or written English, the verb agrees with the first item in the list, even if later items are plural:

  • There is a bank, two shops and a park on this street.
  • There are two shops, a bank and a park on this street.

In everyday speech, people very often say there's before any list, even a plural one. You will hear this constantly, and it is normal spoken English — but write the agreeing form.1

4Short forms

Full formShort formWhere to use it
there isthere'sSpeaking and informal writing. Very common.
there are—There is no short form. Always say there are.
there is notthere isn'tThe usual negative in speech.
there are notthere aren'tThe usual plural negative in speech.
The short form never goes at the end In a short answer, say the full words: “Yes, there is.” — never “Yes, there's.” English does not end a sentence on a contraction. The same is true of it's: we say “Yes, it is.”1

5Not it is, and not it has

Many languages use the verb have where English uses there is. This produces two of the most common sentences learners say, and both sound wrong to an English ear.

not correctIt has a park in my town.
correctThere is a park in my town.
not naturalHas your town a hospital?
correctIs there a hospital in your town?

My town has a hospital is not wrong English — it is a different structure, where my town is the subject. But it cannot be turned into a question the same way, and it is not what we are practicing. When you are describing what exists in a place, reach for there is and there are.

The difference in one line There is introduces something new. It is says more about something we have already mentioned.

There is a new cafe on Garden Street. It is very small, but it is always full.

6In the past: there was, there were

The structure does not change. Only the verb moves into the past.

Singular and uncountablePlural
PositiveThere was a market here.There were two schools.
NegativeThere wasn't any water.There weren't any shops.
QuestionWas there a market?Were there any shops?
Short answerYes, there was. / No, there wasn't.Yes, there were. / No, there weren't.

For B1+: the future works the same way — There will be a new bridge next year. Note that it is will be for both singular and plural.

7Prepositions of place: in, on, at

These three small words carry most of the work. The rule is about the kind of place, not the size of it.2

WordThe ideaExamples
in inside a bigger space in the box · in my bag · in the kitchen
in Kabul · in Afghanistan · in the countryside
in a car · in a taxi
on on a surface, or along a line on the table · on the wall · on the floor
on Garden Street · on the river · on the third floor
on the bus · on a train · on a plane
at at an exact point, or at an activity at home · at work · at school
at the bus stop · at the door · at the top
at 15 Garden Street · at a party
The three that catch everyone in a car, but on a bus, a train or a plane. You sit in something small that you climb into, and on something large that you walk onto.

on a street, but at a house number: She lives on Garden Street.   She lives at 15 Garden Street.2

in a city, but at a place in the city you go to for a reason: in Kabul   at the university.

8Saying where something is

These phrases tell us where one thing is in relation to another. Read them against the street below.

at the end ofThe park is at the end of Garden Street.
onAll eight places are on Garden Street.

In front of, behind, and the rest

These describe what is around a single building rather than along a street.

PhraseMeaning and example
in front ofon the front side of it, outside — There is a tree in front of the library.
behindon the back side — There is a garden behind the library.
oppositefacing it, with something in between — The bank is opposite the school.
under / belowlower than it — There is a river under the bridge.
above / overhigher than it — There is a flat above the shop.
inside / outsideThere are a lot of people inside the market.
across from, opposite, in front of across from and opposite mean exactly the same thing: on the other side of something.3 In American English, across from is the more usual choice; in British English you will hear opposite more often. Both are correct, and you should recognize both.

In front of is different. A bus stop in front of the hospital is on the hospital's own side of the street. A bus stop across from the hospital is on the far side, with the road in between.
