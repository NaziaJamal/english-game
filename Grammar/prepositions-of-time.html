Prepositions of Time: at, on, in

Week 3 — Daily Life & Time  ·  Saying when something happens  ·  American English

1The main idea

We use at, on and in to say when something happens. There is a simple way to remember which one to choose: think about how long the time is.[1]

at a point in time — the shortest
  • at 7 o'clock
  • at noon
  • at midnight
  • at night
  • at lunchtime
on days and dates
  • on Monday
  • on Fridays
  • on May 5th
  • on my birthday
  • on the weekend
in longer periods — the longest
  • in the morning
  • in May
  • in the summer
  • in 2026
  • in two weeks
One easy way to remember The time gets longer as you move along: at a moment → on a day → in a month, a season or a year.

2AT — a point in time

Use at for clock times and for short, exact moments.[1]

Use it forExamples
Clock timesThe class starts at four o'clock.  I wake up at 6:30.
noon and midnightWe have lunch at noon.  The store closes at midnight.
nightI read at night.
MealtimesWe talk at breakfast.  She calls me at lunchtime.
Short expressionsat the moment, at the same time, at that time

3ON — days and dates

Use on for one particular day, for days that repeat, and for dates.[1]

Use it forExamples
Days of the weekI have class on Monday.
Repeated daysShe works on Fridays.  (= every Friday)
DatesMy birthday is on May 5th.  We met on July 12, 2024.
Special dayson my birthday, on New Year's Day, on our anniversary
The weekendI rest on the weekend.  (American English)
A day + part of the dayI'll call you on Monday morning.
Important — day plus part of the day We normally say in the morning. But when we name the day as well, we use on:
CorrectI study in the morning.
CorrectI study on Saturday morning.
Not correctI study in Saturday morning.

4IN — longer periods

Use in for parts of the day, months, seasons, years and other long periods.[1]

Use it forExamples
Parts of the dayin the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening
MonthsMy sister was born in May.
SeasonsIt's very hot in the summer.
Years and decadesWe moved here in 2024.  in the 1990s
Future periodsI'll see you in two weeks.  (= after two weeks)

5The three traps

Trap 1 — at night We say in the morning, in the afternoon and in the evening — but at night. This does not follow the pattern, so it is worth learning as a fixed phrase.
Not correctI read in the night.
CorrectI read at night.
Trap 2 — the day wins When a day and a part of the day come together, on wins.
Not correctWe have class in Friday evening.
CorrectWe have class on Friday evening.
Trap 3 — the weekend In American English we say on the weekend.
In British English you will hear at the weekend. Both are correct in their own variety — choose one and be consistent.[2]

6When you need no preposition at all

Some time words never take at, on or in. This is a very common mistake, so look at this list carefully.[3]

These take no prepositionExample
every day, every week, every yearI study English every day.
today, tomorrow, yesterdayI'll call you tomorrow.
this / last / next + time wordWe met last week.  She starts next month.
all day, all morning, all weekIt rained all day.
Not correctI study English in every day.
CorrectI study English every day.
Not correctI will see you on next week.
CorrectI will see you next week.
Good to know In everyday American speech, people often leave out on before a day: “I'll see you Monday.” This is normal in conversation. Keep on when you write.

7Common mistakes

Not correctCorrect
I wake up in 6 o'clock.I wake up at 6 o'clock.
I study in the night.I study at night.
My birthday is in May 5th.My birthday is on May 5th.
We have class at Monday.We have class on Monday.
She was born on 1998.She was born in 1998.
I rest in the weekend.I rest on the weekend.
I go there in every day.I go there every day.

Remember at a moment → on a day → in a longer period. And the one that breaks the pattern: at night.
