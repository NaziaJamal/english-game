Week 4, Session 1 — Food and Shopping

Mixed level (A2–B1+)  ·  90 minutes  ·  Online (Google Meet)  ·  ESA format
On this page:

Lesson at a glance

Aim: Students talk about food, shop for it, and ask about quantities and prices.

Target language: countable and uncountable nouns; some and any; much, many and a lot of; How much…? and How many…?; quantity phrases (a kilo of, a bottle of, a loaf of).

Recycled: present simple and the third-person –s; have got from Week 2 (“We haven't got any sugar”) — both appear naturally and need no re-teaching.

Materials: Google Meet with chat and breakout rooms; the student worksheet (sent by link before class); slides.

Skills: speaking and listening (primary); reading and writing (supporting).

How the worksheet fits the lesson The worksheet is organised by activity type, and each part belongs to a different stage of this plan: Part A (controlled) in Study · Part B (semi-controlled) at the end of Study · Part C (individual writing) as homework or for fast finishers · Part D (pair and group work) in Activate · Part E (class discussion) in Activate or Wrap-up.

Before class

  • Send students the worksheet link the day before and ask them to open it (not complete it) before class.
  • Have a real item or two beside you if you can — a bag of rice, a bottle of water. Holding something up is the fastest way to teach a quantity phrase.
  • Decide your breakout pairs in advance. Pair a stronger student with a quieter one.
ENGAGEFood vocabulary, fast and personal
15 min

1 · Three things you ate yesterday 6 min · whole class

A quick round that fills the room with the vocabulary you need, before you teach anything.

  • Each student types three foods they ate yesterday into the chat. One or two words each — no sentences.
  • Read some aloud. Group them on screen as they come: things you can count on one side, things you cannot on the other — but do not explain why yet.
  • Ask: “Why did I put these here and these there?” Take guesses. Then move on without confirming.
Why this works Students supply the vocabulary themselves, so it is already familiar when the grammar arrives. The silent sorting plants the countable/uncountable question in their minds a full ten minutes before you answer it.

2 · What am I cooking? 9 min · whole class

  • Show a short shopping list on screen, with quantities: a kilo of rice · two onions · some oil · a little salt · 500g of lamb.
  • Students guess the dish. Let them argue about it — this is the fun part.
  • Then ask each student to type a shopping list for a dish they love, without naming the dish. Read two or three out and let the class guess.
Listen for this Students will naturally reach for quantity words here and get some of them wrong (“two rice”, “many sugar”). Note two or three. You will use them in the Study phase as real examples from the room, which lands far better than invented ones.
STUDYThree short blocks, each with practice
30 min

3 · Countable and uncountable 12 min · whole class, then Worksheet A1

  • Now answer the question from the warm-up. Put two columns on screen and sort the food words together.
  • Draw out the rule from the students rather than stating it: “Can I say one rice, two rices? Can I say one apple, two apples?”

Expect this question Someone will ask about fruit, chicken or coffee. Answer simply: many foods can be both — chicken is uncountable as meat (“some chicken”) and countable as an animal (“two chickens”). Do not go further than that at this level.

4 · some / any · much / many 12 min · whole class, then Worksheet A2–A4

On the board
WordWhen we use itExample
somepositive sentences; offers and requestsI need some milk.  Would you like some tea?
anynegatives and questionsWe haven't got any eggs.  Do you have any rice?
muchuncountable — mostly negatives and questionsThere isn't much milk.  How much sugar?

a lot ofboth — especially in positive sentencesWe have a lot of rice.  She bought a lot of apples.
  • Drill the two questions hard, because students will need them in the role-play: How much…? with uncountable, How many…? with countable plural.
  • Flag the one that is not about quantity at all: How much is it? means what is the price. Students use this constantly and often say “How many is it?”
  • Students do Worksheet A2, A3 and A4. Check together.
Core · A2Use some, any, How much and How many correctly. Treat a lot of as the safe option for positive sentences.
Stretch · B1+Add a few (countable) and a little (uncountable), and use much and many accurately in negatives.

5 · Semi-controlled practice 6 min · pairs · Worksheet B

  • Breakout pairs. Students do Worksheet B1 (quantity phrases) and B2 (the market dialogue) together, saying the answers aloud rather than only writing.
  • Ask one pair to read the completed dialogue to the class as a performance. This doubles as your check.
Monitoring The two errors to collect are “How many is it?” for price, and some used in questions. Note who says them and fix both at the end for the whole class, without naming anyone.
ACTIVATEReal shopping, real decisions
35 min

6 · Role-play: at the market 18 min · pairs · Worksheet D1

The heart of the lesson. Every sentence they need was taught in the last half hour.

  • Breakout pairs. Student A is the customer, Student B is the shopkeeper. Their role cards are on the worksheet, and each card tells them something the other does not know.
  • First run (6 min): follow the cards. The customer must buy everything on the list and ask the price.
  • Swap roles and run it again (6 min), with a twist: the shopkeeper has run out of two items and must offer something else.
  • Back together (6 min): one or two pairs perform. The class listens for one thing — how the customer asked the price.
Why the second run matters The first run is reading the cards. The second, with something missing, forces real negotiation — “We haven't got any tomatoes. Would you like some peppers?” That is where the language becomes theirs.
Core · A2Use the sentence starters printed on the role card.
Stretch · B1+Cover the starters. Add a complication of your own: bargain over the price, or ask for something the shop does not sell.

7 · Group task: a meal for the class 17 min · groups of three or four · Worksheet D2

  • Groups of three or four in breakout rooms. Each group plans one meal to cook for the whole class, and fills in the shopping table on the worksheet.
  • They must agree on the dish, list what they need with quantities, and be ready to say why they chose it.
  • Back together: each group presents its meal in about a minute. The class votes for the one they would most like to eat.
Keep the budget light A budget makes groups negotiate, which is good for speaking — but money can be a sensitive subject. Frame it loosely: “Keep it simple and affordable” rather than naming a figure, unless your group suggests one themselves.

8 · Class discussion if time allows · Worksheet E

Use these if the groups finish early, or save them for Session 2. They move students from transactional language into opinion.

  • Is it better to cook at home or buy food ready-made? Why?
  • What food from your region would you want everyone in the world to try?
  • Who taught you to cook? What was the first thing you learned?
WRAP-UPConsolidate and close
10 min
  • Put one summary slide up: some / any on one line, much / many / a lot of on the next, How much / How many on the third.
  • Fix the two or three errors you collected, as “a few things I heard today”, with no names.
  • Praise specific sentences, with names. Praise is safe to attribute; corrections are not.
  • Exit ticket — in the chat: (1) one thing you have a lot of in your kitchen; (2) one thing you haven't got any of; (3) one question with How much or How many.
  • Homework: Worksheet Part C — either writing task. Post it in Google Classroom before the next class.
Looking ahead Session 2 this week takes the same language into a bigger task — comparing prices and places to shop — and is the natural home for the Part E discussion questions if you do not reach them today.

B1 · Quantity phrases

a bottle of water  ·  a kilo of rice  ·  a loaf of bread  ·  a cup of tea  ·  a packet of biscuits  ·  a bag of flour

B2 · At the market — the dialogue

1 some  ·  2 much  ·  3 any  ·  4 some  ·  5 How many  ·  6 How much

B3 · Write the question

  1. How much rice do you need?
  2. How many eggs do we need? (also fine: …do you need?)
  3. How much is it? (also fine: How much does it cost?)
  4. How many people are there? (also fine: How many people are coming?)

Accept any grammatical question that fits the answer. The point is the How much / How many choice, not the exact wording.

C, D and E

Answers will vary. When you mark Part C, check for three things: a / an not used with uncountable nouns, any in negatives and questions, and How much with uncountable nouns and prices.

Running this lesson online

  • Hold things up. A real bag of rice on camera teaches “a kilo of rice” faster than any slide. Ask students to fetch one thing from their kitchen for the warm-up if they can.
  • Use the chat for the controlled parts. A1 to A4 go quickly if students type answers rather than taking turns speaking — and you can see everyone's answer at once.
  • Role-play needs breakout rooms. Doing it one pair at a time in front of the whole class wastes most of the lesson and frightens the quiet students.
  • Give the listeners a job during the performances: count how many times the customer uses How much or How many.
  • Food is a safe, warm topic for this group — it invites pride and memory rather than difficulty. Let the conversation wander a little when it does.

“Little by little, a little becomes a lot.”
